What the options mean

How the fly learns

Bio-RL
The simulated brain learns the way a real brain does. After each key press, a burst of the reward chemical dopamine strengthens the connections that led to a hit, and a punishment burst weakens the ones that led to a mistake.
Deep-RL
The brain stays frozen and never changes. A small, separate AI program watches the brain's output and learns which key to press. Most online "fly brain plays a game" demos work this way.

How the brain is wired

Real wiring
Connected exactly like a real fruit fly's brain, using a published map of its neurons.
Shuffled wiring
The same kinds of cells and the same number of connections, but who connects to whom is scrambled. This checks whether the fly's exact wiring matters.
Random network
The same number of neurons and connections, wired completely at random, so there is no brain-like structure at all.
Dopamine blocked
Bio-RL with its reward and punishment signal switched off. If it stops learning, dopamine is what does the learning.

In short: the brain-style learner needed dopamine and brain-like structure to learn, but the fly's exact wiring made no difference in this test. It also beat the frozen-brain AI approach on the real and shuffled brains.

 

 

Each grey dot is the cell body of one of 139,662 neurons in a fly's brain and nerve cord (Janelia male CNS), seen from above with the head at the top. The empty band below the brain is the neck: only nerve fibres pass through it, so it has no cell bodies to draw. The simulated neurons come from the FlyWire brain and are drawn at a cell of the same type. When a note falls into a lane, that lane's visual neurons fire, then Kenyon cells, then the output neurons that choose a lane; each flashes as it spikes. For the Bio-RL fly, a hit releases reward dopamine and a mistake releases punishment dopamine, which strengthen or weaken the synapses that just fired.
